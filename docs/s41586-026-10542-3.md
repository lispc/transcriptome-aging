# Universal Transcriptomic Hallmarks of Mammalian Ageing and Mortality

> **Original Paper:** Tyshkovskiy A. et al., *Nature* 654, 173–187 (2026). DOI: [10.1038/s41586-026-10542-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10542-3)

---

## Overview

This study presents the most comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of mammalian ageing to date, integrating **over 11,000 transcriptomes** from **more than 25 tissues** across **four mammalian species** (mouse, rat, macaque, and human). The core achievement is the development of accurate, interpretable, and cross-species "transcriptomic clocks" (tAge) that can measure not just chronological age, but more importantly, **expected mortality**—a proxy for molecular health status and biological damage accumulation.

The key insight is that ageing and mortality leave conserved, modular signatures in gene expression that can be quantified, decomposed into specific cellular pathways, and used to predict lifespan-modulating interventions, time to death, chronic diseases, and rejuvenation.

---

## Why This Matters

Ageing is characterized by systemic damage accumulation and exponentially increasing mortality. While numerous genetic, dietary, and pharmacological interventions can modulate lifespan—from caloric restriction and rapamycin to progeria models—there has been no unified molecular framework to measure "biological age" across species, tissues, and interventions in an interpretable way.

Existing biomarkers have limitations:
- **DNA methylation clocks** often lack mechanistic interpretability and are largely restricted to human blood.
- **Transcriptomic clocks** offer better functional annotation but previously lacked cross-species validation and mortality prediction power.

This work bridges that gap by creating mortality-aware transcriptomic clocks that generalize across mammals while retaining pathway-level interpretability.

## Study Design & Data

### The ITP Cohort: A Pharmacological Lifespan Map
The study leveraged the **NIA Interventions Testing Program (ITP)**, which since 2004 has tested over 50 compounds for lifespan extension in genetically heterogeneous mice. The authors sequenced livers from **170 old mice** (22 months) exposed to **20 different pharmacological interventions**—including proven lifespan extenders like rapamycin, acarbose, and canagliflozin—plus young controls.

### Multi-Species Meta-Dataset
- **Rodents:** 4,539 samples across 26 tissues (liver, brain, muscle, heart, kidney, blood, skin, etc.) from mice and rats, spanning natural ageing and 79 interventions.
- **Primates:** 2,623 crab-eating macaque samples (30+ tissues) and 4,003 human samples (blood, brain, muscle, skin).

For each sample, the authors computed not just chronological age, but **expected mortality** using Gompertz survival models fitted to published survival data, and **normalized age** (chronological age divided by expected maximum lifespan).

## Key Results

### 1. Building the Clocks: Age vs. Mortality

The team trained several types of elastic net regression models:

| Clock Type | What It Predicts | Key Property |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronological clock** | Relative chronological age | Tracks natural ageing well, but misses lifespan-extending interventions |
| **Normalized-age clock** | Age as % of expected lifespan | Integrates ageing + intervention effects |
| **Mortality clock** | Expected log hazard rate | Best at distinguishing both lifespan-extending and lifespan-shortening interventions |
| **Lifespan clock** | Maximum lifespan directly | Less accurate than mortality clock for intervention classification |

**Critical finding:** The mortality clock outperformed even the dedicated lifespan clock in predicting whether an intervention extends or shortens life. This suggests that integrating chronological ageing with health deterioration signals creates a more powerful biomarker than either alone.

The clocks achieved excellent accuracy (test set R² ≈ 0.91–0.92, Pearson's r ≈ 0.95–0.96) and generalized well across tissues via leave-one-tissue-out validation (median r ≈ 0.86–0.88), confirming that systemic ageing signatures are shared across organs.

### 2. Conserved Ageing Signatures Across Mammals

Ageing-related gene expression changes are remarkably conserved:

**Consistently UP with age / mortality:**
- **CDKN1A** (p21): cell cycle arrest, senescence marker
- **LGALS3** (Galectin-3): inflammation, immune modulation
- **GPNMB**: inflammation-associated glycoprotein
- **CCL2, CCL5**: chemokines, immune recruitment
- **CASP1**: inflammasome component

**Consistently DOWN with age / mortality:**
- **Nrep**: neuronal regeneration and differentiation
- **COL1A1, COL3A1**: collagen, extracellular matrix (ECM)
- **SPARC**: ECM regulator, wound healing

Pathway analysis revealed consistent patterns:
- **Upregulated with ageing:** Interferon signaling, TNF-α/NF-κB inflammation, complement/coagulation, p53 pathway, hypoxia response
- **Downregulated with ageing:** Oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial translation, fatty acid metabolism, DNA repair, collagen formation

Cross-species comparisons (rodents vs. primates) showed positive correlations in age-associated gene slopes, confirming universal mammalian ageing architecture.

### 3. Ageing Happens in (Almost) Every Cell Type

Using single-cell RNA-seq data from the **Tabula Muris Senis** atlas (49 cell types from 9 tissues), the authors found:
- **48/49 cell types** showed significant positive correlation between chronological age and tAge predicted by the mortality clock.
- Even **stem cell populations** (mesenchymal stem cells, hematopoietic stem cells) exhibited pro-mortality expression shifts, demonstrating that ageing is not confined to differentiated cells.
- Shared pro-ageing genes across cell types included *Cdkn1a*, *Lgals3*, *Casp1*, and *S100a8*.

### 4. A Modular Architecture of Ageing

Instead of treating ageing as a monolithic process, the authors used weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to decompose ageing into **28 functional modules** (clusters of co-regulated genes). Each module represents a distinct cellular subsystem that ages with its own trajectory.

**Major modules include:**
1. **Innate immunity / Inflammation**
2. **Interferon signaling**
3. **Adaptive immunity / T cell signaling**
4. **Mitochondrial translation / Oxidative phosphorylation**
5. **Chromatin modification** (two distinct modules)
6. **ECM organization / EMT**
7. **Fatty acid metabolism**
8. **Cell cycle / DNA replication**
9. **Heat stress response**
10. **Translation / Ribosome**

The authors then built **module-specific clocks**—mini-clocks trained only on genes from one module. This enables asking: "Which cellular subsystem is ageing fastest in this condition?"

### 5. Different Interventions Target Different Modules

This modular decomposition revealed that lifespan-modulating interventions have **pathway-specific signatures**:

| Intervention / Condition | Primary Module Affected | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic diseases** (AD, stroke, CKD, NASH) | Inflammation, interferon | Accelerated |
| **Caloric restriction** | Mitochondrial metabolism, lipid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation | Reversed (younger) |
| **LPS (acute inflammation)** | Innate immunity, interferon | Accelerated |
| **Klotho knockout (progeria)** | Respiration, mitochondrial translation, cholesterol/mTOR | Accelerated |
| **γ-irradiation** | Chromatin, interferon, mitochondrial translation, cytoskeleton | Accelerated |
| **Cell reprogramming (iPSC)** | ECM/EMT | Reversed (younger) |
| **Heterochronic parabiosis** | Inflammation, interferon, p53, apoptosis | Reversed (younger) |

**Key insight:** Not all "ageing" is the same. Klotho deficiency accelerates metabolic ageing without strongly activating inflammation, whereas chronic diseases primarily inflame the immune modules. Caloric restriction broadly rejuvenates metabolic modules. This means "biological age" should be viewed as a **vector**, not a scalar.

### 6. In Vitro Validation: Damage and Rejuvenation

The clocks were applied to cellular models:

**Damage accumulation increases tAge:**
- Primary human fibroblasts in culture show linear tAge increase over time (replicative senescence).
- Metabolic stressors (oligomycin, 2-deoxyglucose) accelerate tAge.
- γ-irradiation of mouse and naked mole rat fibroblasts increases tAge across multiple modules.

**Rejuvenation decreases tAge:**
- **hTERT immortalization** abolishes the tAge increase in WI-38 fibroblasts, proving clocks measure damage, not mere time.
- **iPSC reprogramming** reduces tAge, with the strongest rejuvenation signal in the ECM/EMT module (consistent with mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition during reprogramming).

Interestingly, γ-irradiation and hTERT did **not** alter DNA methylation age, suggesting transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks capture **complementary aspects** of ageing—expression reflects dynamic functional states, while methylation reflects longer-term stochastic changes.

### 7. Chronic Diseases Share Mortality Hallmarks

Nine rodent chronic disease models were analyzed:
- **Alzheimer's disease** (brain, pineal gland)
- **Chronic kidney disease** (kidney)
- **Diabetic nephropathy** (kidney)
- **Ischaemic stroke** (brain, heart)
- **NASH** (liver)
- **Hepatocellular carcinoma** (liver)

**Most diseases accelerated mortality tAge.** The most consistent cross-disease pro-mortality genes were **Gpnmb, Cdkn1a, and Lgals3**.

**HCC was exceptional:** tumors showed **lower** mortality tAge than controls due to proliferation and dedifferentiation (youth-like features), but simultaneously exhibited pro-ageing inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. This highlights that "youthfulness" in one dimension (cell cycle) can coexist with "ageing" in another (inflammation).

### 8. Predicting Human Mortality

In the **Framingham Heart Study** (FHS) blood data:
- The universal mortality clock predicted time to death with performance **comparable to second-generation DNA methylation clocks** (DunedinPACE, PhenoAge, YingDamAge).
- It **outperformed existing human transcriptomic chronological clocks** (Peters clock, RNAAgeCalc).
- **Age accuracy and mortality prediction are largely uncoupled:** some clocks are great at guessing age but poor at predicting death, and vice versa.

Transcriptomic and DNA methylation clock predictions were positively correlated in human blood, with the **chromatin-modification module clock** showing the strongest correlation with epigenetic clocks—providing mechanistic evidence linking chromatin state to epigenetic ageing.

In **UK Biobank plasma proteomics** (50,000+ participants), three key proteins—**GPNMB, CDKN1A, and LGALS3**—were positively associated with:
- All-cause mortality
- Cardiovascular disease, stroke, heart failure
- Diabetes, kidney failure, liver cirrhosis
- Risk factors: obesity, hypertension, sleep disorders

This validates that the transcriptomic biomarkers identified in rodents translate to human protein-level mortality and disease associations.

### 9. Rejuvenation During Early Embryogenesis

Analysing mouse embryogenesis from zygote to birth:
- Both chronological and mortality clocks show a **U-shaped tAge trajectory**.
- tAge decreases from fertilization to approximately **embryonic day 10 (E10)**, reaching a minimum ("ground zero"), then increases thereafter.
- This rejuvenation phase is conserved across nearly all cell lineages (mesoderm, endoderm, ectoderm) in scRNA-seq data from E6.5–E8.5 embryos.
- Shared rejuvenation signatures across embryogenesis, heterochronic parabiosis, and caloric restriction include downregulation of **Cdkn1a** and inflammatory factors (**Ccl5, S100a9, Lgals3**).

## Summary & Implications

This study establishes that:

1. **Mammalian ageing has universal transcriptomic signatures** conserved across species, tissues, and cell types.
2. **Mortality-associated biomarkers** are more informative than chronological age clocks for assessing health and predicting lifespan-modulating interventions.
3. **Ageing is modular**—different cellular subsystems (inflammation, metabolism, chromatin, ECM) can age at different rates and be differentially targeted by interventions.
4. **Transcriptomic clocks complement epigenetic clocks**—they capture dynamic functional states and offer superior interpretability through gene- and pathway-level readouts.
5. **Core mortality biomarkers** (CDKN1A, LGALS3, GPNMB) are validated from rodents through to human plasma proteomics and predict death, chronic disease, and cardiovascular outcomes.

The framework moves us toward a **subsystem-resolved** understanding of biological age, where instead of asking "How old is this organism?", we can ask "Which parts of this organism are ageing, and can we target them?"
